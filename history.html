HISTORICAL CHAPTERS

The development of Delhi

Architecture is only part of it. This is also a history of women patrons, craftspeople, traders, migration and loss.

01BEFORE 1526
Mehrauli · Siri · Tughlaqabad · Firozabad

Delhi before Mughal rule

Delhi’s history does not begin with the Red Fort. Older settlements and capitals developed around Mehrauli, Siri, Tughlaqabad and Firozabad. The Qutb complex preserves major remains of the Delhi Sultanate, including the early thirteenth-century Qutb Minar.

At Purana Qila, archaeology reveals settlement long before its surviving sixteenth-century walls. Tradition links the site with the Mahabharata’s Indraprastha; that is an association, not archaeological proof of the epic city. “Seven cities of Delhi” is a storytelling shorthand with varying lists. Think overlapping places, rather than seven cities replacing one another.

021526–1556
Purana Qila · Dinpanah

The early Mughals and Dinpanah

Babur’s victory at Panipat in 1526 opened the Mughal era. Humayun began Dinpanah at Purana Qila in 1533, but Sher Shah Sur displaced him in 1540. Humayun recovered Delhi and Agra in 1555.

Purana Qila carries a shared Mughal–Sur story. Its surviving buildings cannot all be assigned confidently to a single ruler. The Yamuna once bordered its eastern side; today the river lies farther east. Water helped shape where the city was built, how it looked and how it worked.

031560s–EARLY 1600s
Nizamuddin · Humayun’s Tomb

Mughal gardens and imperial patronage

Delhi remained important when the Mughal court operated elsewhere. Agra, Fatehpur Sikri and Lahore were also major centres: Delhi was not the dynasty’s continuous sole capital.

Built around 1570, Humayun’s Tomb made the monumental garden-tomb a defining Mughal form and influenced later designs, including the Taj Mahal. Art grew through exchange too. Akbar’s workshops brought Persian artists into contact with Indian Hindu and Muslim artists. Patrons, designers and craftspeople belong in the story alongside emperors.

041638–1650s
Red Fort · the walled city

The foundation of Shahjahanabad

Shah Jahan’s new city moved the imperial centre north along the Yamuna. The city project began in the late 1630s; construction of the Red Fort ran from 1639 to 1648. This was a palace complex as well as a defensive enclosure, with gardens, pavilions and linked water channels.

Outside the palace, streets, markets, places of worship and residential quarters formed Shahjahanabad—the historic core now commonly called Old Delhi. Its architecture drew on multiple traditions. The achievement was both an imperial setting and a city where people lived, worked and traded.

05THE 17TH-CENTURY CITY
Chandni Chowk · Fatehpuri

Urban life, trade and cultural patronage

Jahanara Begum, Shah Jahan’s daughter, was an important patron of Chandni Chowk’s street, gardens and caravanserai. Fatehpuri Begum commissioned the mosque at its western end in 1650. Women belong near the centre of Delhi’s building story.

Courtyard houses, lanes, markets and commercial enclosures made the city work. Today’s bazaars and places of worship carry layers of adaptation. That does not mean every present shop, recipe or institution survives unchanged from Mughal times: continuity also includes rebuilding, migration and new arrivals.

061739–1858
Old Delhi · the Ridge

Political change and the events of 1857

Nadir Shah’s invasion in 1739 brought massacre and plunder. As Mughal authority weakened, other powers competed for influence. British East India Company control arrived in 1803; the emperor remained in Delhi with sharply restricted power.

In 1857 Delhi became a centre of the uprising, with Bahadur Shah Zafar as its symbolic imperial figure. British forces retook the city in September, followed by looting and killings. The Red Fort’s later military use changed its spaces. Zafar’s trial and exile followed in 1858, ending the Mughal court’s place in the city.

071911–1931
New Delhi · the ceremonial axis

The planning and construction of New Delhi

The British announced the transfer of their Indian capital from Calcutta to Delhi in December 1911. The new administrative city was planned and built south of Shahjahanabad. Broad avenues and ceremonial spaces created a different arrangement from the older city’s dense lanes.

Edwin Lutyens and Herbert Baker were central to its design, while construction also depended on Indian labour and craftsmanship. New Delhi was inaugurated in 1931. Keep those dates distinct: 1911 was the announcement; 1931 marked the inauguration of the new capital.

081947–TODAY
Resettlement · migration · Metro connections

Partition, migration and modern Delhi

Independence and Partition brought hope and profound upheaval. Refugees arriving in Delhi built new neighbourhoods and livelihoods. Muslims leaving or seeking protection also experienced displacement and loss; Purana Qila served as a camp. Housing, employment and migration continued to remake the city.

The Metro added new everyday connections. Public services began between Shahdara and Tis Hazari on 25 December 2002, after inauguration the previous day. Today’s Delhi brings historic quarters, villages and planned neighbourhoods into one urban life. Conservation in Nizamuddin shows how heritage can also sustain living communities and traditional crafts.
